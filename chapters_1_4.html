SEX TAX

“Do not give your strength to women, or your ways to that which destroys kings.” — Proverbs 31:3

AUTHOR’S NOTE

“There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death.”

— Proverbs 14:12

Have you heard of the Liberal Paradox? It’s sometimes called Sen’s Paradox, after Amartya Sen’s 1970 paper in the Journal of Political Economy, “The Impossibility of a Paretian Liberal.”

Suppose you are looking forward to reading this book. Yes, it’s hot and heavy, but you know you’ll enjoy it. You think there’s nothing wrong with books like these and it’s healthy and normal for adults to read them. Following Sen, let’s call you Lewd.

Or suppose you think that books like these are indecent, unhealthy, abnormal, and should be burned unread. Again, following Sen, let’s call you Prude.

And yes — this book is, quite literally, one of those books. Lewd would press it into your hands. Prude would light the match.

Now suppose Lewd would rather convince Prude to read it than read it herself. She thinks it’ll broaden his horizons and help him live a more enjoyable life. That’s the kind of generous girl Lewd is.

Let’s write “>” for “prefers to.”

Lewd’s preferences: Prude Reads > Lewd Reads > Burn the Book.

As for Prude, he thinks it’s simply wrong to enjoy reading such books. But if the book isn’t to be burned, he’d rather read it himself than let Lewd read it. That’s the kind of protective guy Prude is.

Prude’s preferences: Burn the Book > Prude Reads > Lewd Reads.

Who should read it? It’s a conundrum, right?

Let’s invent Shrewd: a thoughtful arbiter who happens to have real authority — perhaps elected, perhaps appointed, perhaps simply trusted. She wants what’s best for everyone and is willing to do the work. Should Shrewd make Lewd read this book, make Prude read it, or burn it unread?

Shrewd is reasonable. She respects individual freedom: when a choice concerns only one person, that person’s own preference should decide. So:

— Whose opinion matters for “Lewd Reads” vs. “Burn the Book”? Lewd’s — it’s her book, her time, her eyes. Lewd wants to read it. So Shrewd ranks Lewd Reads > Burn the Book.

— Whose opinion matters for “Burn the Book” vs. “Prude Reads”? Prude’s — nobody should force him to read what he finds indecent. Prude would rather see it burned. So Shrewd ranks Burn the Book > Prude Reads.

Put them together, and Shrewd’s judgment is: Lewd Reads > Burn the Book > Prude Reads. Therefore Lewd reads the book.

So what’s the problem?

The problem is that both Prude and Lewd regard this outcome as worse than “Prude reads.” Lewd wanted Prude to read it more than she wanted to read it herself, remember? And Prude preferred to read it himself rather than let Lewd enjoy it. Both got the outcome neither wanted.

This is just one example, but Sen proved something general. To see why, consider what happens when there is no Shrewd at all — when each person simply decides only their own personal matter.

The question “does Lewd read?” is her own personal matter — only her preference counts. Lewd wants to read. So she reads.

The question “does Prude read?” is his own personal matter — only his preference counts. Prude doesn’t want to. So he doesn’t.

Result: Lewd reads, Prude doesn’t.

But look at what both of them actually prefer when comparing the two complete outcomes:

— Lewd prefers “Prude Reads” over “Lewd Reads” — she wanted to convince him more than she wanted to read herself.

— Prude prefers “Prude Reads” over “Lewd Reads” — if anyone has to read it, he’d rather it be him containing it than her enjoying it.

Both unanimously prefer “Prude reads, Lewd doesn’t.” But the Liberal principle, applied cleanly, gave them “Lewd reads, Prude doesn’t” — the outcome both prefer less.

Why does this happen? When each person decides only their own personal matter, they ignore their preferences about other people’s choices. Lewd has an opinion about whether Prude reads. Prude has an opinion about whether Lewd reads. The Liberal principle silences both of those — it says your only vote is on your own life.

That sounds fair. But it means meta-preferences — preferences about others — get zeroed out. And sometimes those meta-preferences point at an outcome everyone would prefer.

In other words: as individuals choosing only their own affairs, they get one outcome. As a group, they would unanimously prefer a different one.

To see this as a logical contradiction rather than a story, label the three possible outcomes:

— X = Lewd reads, Prude doesn’t.

— Y = Prude reads, Lewd doesn’t.

— Z = the book is burnt, nobody reads.

The preferences:

— Lewd ranks: Y > X > Z (she most wants Prude to read; second, herself; burning is the worst).

— Prude ranks: Z > Y > X (he most wants it burnt; second, that he read it himself; her reading it is the worst).

Apply the Pareto principle (when everyone agrees that A is better than B, the group ranks A above B):

Both Lewd and Prude rank Y over X. So Pareto says: Y > X.

Apply the Liberal principle (each person is decisive on their own slice — the comparisons that hinge on their own action):

— Comparing X to Z is Lewd’s slice — the only difference is whether she reads. Her vote rules. Lewd ranks X above Z. So Liberal says: X > Z.

— Comparing Z to Y is Prude’s slice — the only difference is whether he reads. His vote rules. Prude ranks Z above Y. So Liberal says: Z > Y.

— Combining by transitivity: X > Z > Y. So Liberal says: X > Y.

Now look at what you have:

— Pareto says: Y > X.

— Liberal says: X > Y.

These contradict. Direct logical contradiction — not “they sometimes pull apart” or “they trade off,” but two rules that say opposite things about the same comparison. You cannot honor both simultaneously without producing a logical impossibility.

Sen proved this isn’t a quirk of Lewd and Prude’s particular preferences. It follows from any setup with enough preference structure — and for any decision procedure whatsoever (voting rule, authority, market, algorithm). No procedure can simultaneously satisfy both of these:

— The Pareto principle: if everyone prefers A to B, the group should rank A above B. The minimum bar of honoring unanimous preference.

— The Liberal principle: each person is decisive over their own personal domain.

Any decision procedure that takes individual preferences and produces a group outcome will fail this test. The mathematics rules out the existence of such a procedure, regardless of who or what is doing the aggregating.

So: no system — authority or otherwise — can give you both individual sovereignty over personal matters AND outcomes that respect what everyone unanimously wants. Something has to give. You can have one principle or the other in any given case, but never both, at scale, for everyone.

This isn’t abstract. The book in your hands, unapologetically combining raw passion and thought, is exactly such a test case. The Liberal principle says reading it is your decision alone. The Pareto principle would have you yield to what your community — yourself included — would most prefer overall. As Lewd, Prude, and the math just showed, those two principles can give opposite verdicts on the very same choice. Pick one.

Three lessons. One: talk to each other about your preferences — be open, be sincere; sometimes the individual preferences that involve other people point at a better outcome for everyone than the individual preferences involving only you. Two: don’t expect any authority — government or otherwise — to resolve this paradox in your favor, because no authority can. The mathematics doesn’t allow it. Three — the most important lesson of all — whether you are Lewd, or Prude, or Shrewd: read this book.

What follows is unflinching. Witches, physics, the Kremlin, fire, and the human gaze. A state that has chosen. A protagonist who hasn’t. Lewd will recognize the territory. Prude will feel the heat.

You may — no, you will — learn something you didn’t know before. Something important. And who knows, upon reflection, you might even change your views on what’s actually yours to decide, and what isn’t. The argument is finished. The fire starts on the next page. And above all — enjoy it!

I lower my eyes and think. The professor turns away to examine another student. After a few minutes, the student is dismissed — he rushes out looking straight ahead, which probably means it didn’t go well — and the professor turns back to me.

“The answer is one over p.”

A young woman enters the room and stands near the door.

The professor looks at me with disdain for stating something so obvious, but he asks, “Why?”

“If the probability of getting heads is one half, and we flip the coin a hundred times, we should expect to see about fifty heads. That’s the meaning of probability. And the law of large numbers.”

“The images they found on my phone — I believe they were planted. My phone was remotely disabled the night before my arrest. It flashed, made a sound I’ve never heard, and went dead. I think someone put those images on it before killing it.”

She looks at me steadily. Her expression doesn’t change. I can’t tell if she believes me.

“That’s an unusual claim,” she says. “Do you have any evidence of the remote access?”

“The phone is dead. The FBI took it during my arrest. If someone examines it forensically, they should be able to see when the images were loaded and whether it was done remotely.”

“I’ll note that.” She makes a note. Her pen moves in quick, precise strokes. “Anything else?”

“No. That’s all.”

“You’re charged with transporting child pornography across state lines — which means using the internet to download it onto your device. First-time offenders face a statutory minimum of five years, up to twenty.”

“Five to twenty years? That’s insane.”

“With a plea deal, we could negotiate three to five. That’s the realistic path here.”

“I didn’t do it.”

“Mr. Gabler, the state has a device with images on it, registered to you. Your story about remote access is — “ she pauses, choosing her words — “difficult to substantiate. I’ll do my best, but I want you to understand the situation.”

The enormous smile is back. Its sincerity is no longer a question.

“How do you plead?”

“Not guilty.”

“Good.” She makes another note. “Do you have cash for bail?”

“I have a credit card.”

“This judge won’t accept cards.”

“How much would I need?”

“In a case like this, bail is typically ten thousand. You’d need ten percent — one thousand cash — for a bail company.”

“I don’t have that.”

“Do you have friends or relatives who could post it?”

I think of Jezebel. My parents. I imagine the phone call. I’m in jail. For child pornography. It’s not what you think. Every version of that conversation ends the same way — with a silence worse than any words.

“No,” I say.

“Let’s see what the judge decides. Sometimes bail can be waived.” She pats my hand once, briefly, and leaves.

Another hour. Then the cop leads me through an empty corridor into the courtroom. It’s a large room, mostly empty — a few people scattered in the back rows, several serious-looking figures in the front who are clearly part of the proceedings. A bailiff stands near the side door.

The cop leads me to the front row to sit with Mrs. Pierse. A few seats down, I notice Agent Mizushima. She’s wearing a gray business suit. With her short hair and the severe cut of the jacket, she looks like a young male executive. Beside her sits a gray-haired man I don’t recognize — the assistant U.S. attorney, Mrs. Pierse whispers.

Seeing Mizushima produces a sensation I can’t name. Not anger, though I should be angry — she arrested me. Not gratitude, though she gave me money and advice and, on the shoulder of a New Jersey highway, something else entirely. The feeling is more like vertigo. As if looking at her tilts the floor.

She glances at me once. Her face reveals nothing. She looks away.

“All rise,” announces the bailiff. “The court is now in session, the Honorable Judge Chin presiding.”

Three people enter the elevated platform. The judge — a tall woman in her fifties, composed, unhurried — takes her seat. A clerk with a cardboard box of files sits beside her. A court reporter with a laptop positions himself below.

The judge looks at me. “State your name for the court.”

I stand. Mrs. Pierse stands with me. “Gil Gabler.”

“I am Judge Bao Chin. I am here to inform you of the charges against you, the range of punishment, your right to an attorney, and to set bail. This is not an arraignment. I cannot hear your plea or testimony regarding the alleged offense.”

I nod. My attorney just explained all of this.

“Is your true and correct name Gil Gabler?”

“Yes, Your Honor.”

She reads me my rights. Asks if I understand. Asks if I’m satisfied with my representation. Yes. Yes. What choice do I have?

She asks where I live, with whom, how long. What I do. “I’m a student, Your Honor.”

Have I been arrested before? “No, Your Honor.”

She sets bail at ten thousand dollars and tells me to return tomorrow at three p.m. for arraignment.

“May I pay with a credit card?” I ask.

“No.”

“May I call someone who can bring cash?” I’m ready to call Jezebel now. I’ll endure the silence on the phone if it means not going back.

“It’s too late for that,” says the judge. She moves to the next case.

The bailiff leads me back to the waiting room. The cop with the eyebrows is there.

“Let’s go,” he says. “I’ll take you to the bus. Back to the facility.”

Another night. I survived one. I’ll survive another. But I’m in no hurry to get on that bus.

“Can I ask my attorney one more question?”

He dials his phone. Almost immediately, Mrs. Pierse and her big smile make their appearance.

“I’m glad I haven’t left yet. What’s your question?”

I realize I don’t actually have a question. I just didn’t want to be handcuffed again yet. But I have to say something.

“What’s our defense strategy?”

“Defense strategy?” She repeats it as if I’ve asked her to explain dark matter.

“Yes. What do we tell the judge to get me free?”

“We’ll study the evidence and evaluate similar cases.” She recovers quickly. A professional smile. There’s nothing I can say to extend this conversation.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

She leaves — smile and all. The cop says, “Turn around.” Time to be cuffed again.

Before he touches me, his phone rings. He answers. Speaks briefly. Hangs up.

“Somebody posted your bail,” he says. “You can go.”

I stare at him. “I can go?”

“You’re free. For now.” He smiles — a genuine, warm smile that transforms his face and makes the eyebrows look almost friendly.

I walk out of the waiting room. Down the corridor. Past the metal detector. Through the front door.

Outside, the sun is low and the air smells like asphalt and cut grass. I stand on the courthouse steps and breathe. I don’t know who posted my bail. I don’t know why. I don’t know what happens tomorrow or next week or at the arraignment. I have no phone, no money, and a charge that will follow me like a smell.

But the sun is warm, and I’m not in a cell, and no one is telling me where to stand or sit or turn around, and I stretch my arms wide — both arms, as far as they’ll go — like a man trying to hold the whole sky.

I have no idea where I am or how to get home.

He fitted the wings to his son’s arms. He adjusted the harness. He told him the rules.

“Not too high — the sun will soften the wax. Not too low — the sea spray will soak the feathers. Follow me. Stay in the middle air.”
